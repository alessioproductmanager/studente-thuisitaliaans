The Testing Effect: Quiz Yourself in Italian

9 March 2026

Two students prepare the same Italian vocabulary. One rereads the list four times. The other reads it once, then closes the book and tries to recall it three times, failing plenty along the way. A week later, the second student remembers dramatically more. This is the testing effect, shown most famously by Roediger and Karpicke in 2006, and it may be the single most useful finding a self-learner can steal from cognitive psychology.

The uncomfortable part: during practice, the rereaders felt better and predicted better results for themselves. Rereading is fluent: every line looks familiar, and familiarity feels like knowledge. Retrieval is the opposite: you stare, you blank, you produce the wrong article. It feels like failing. The research is blunt about which feeling to trust: the act of pulling something out of memory is what strengthens it, and ease during practice predicts almost nothing.

Turning study into retrieval

The conversion is mechanical. Anything you currently re-read, re-watch or re-listen can become a self-test:

  • Finished a chapter of a graded reader? Close it and retell the chapter aloud in Italian: retrieval and output in one move.
  • Learned words this morning? Tonight, produce them from the English side, not the Italian side. Recognition is the easy direction; production is the one conversations demand.
  • Studied the imperfetto? Write three sentences about your childhood without looking at the chart, then check.
  • Vocabulary cards? Always answer before flipping. A card you flip immediately is rereading with extra steps.

And crucially: getting it wrong, then seeing the answer, still beats not testing at all. Errors followed by feedback are productive, productive enough that they deserve their own review system.

Why quizzes are the right shape after all

There is an irony here given how this series started. The four strands article argues that app quizzes are only a quarter of the job, and that stands. But within that quarter, the quiz format is exactly right: every gap-fill and every recall prompt is retrieval practice, which is why deliberate study should look like questions, not like highlighted notes. The problem with quiz-only apps is the missing three quarters, not the quizzes.

In ti, retrieval is the default everywhere: twenty-plus exercise types all demand an answer before showing one, the pre-lesson vocabulary check runs with the book closed (see pre-learning vocabulary), each novel chapter ends with three questions answered from memory, and checkpoint tests re-test earlier material rather than just the latest unit. Even the two-minute timed review is retrieval: fast retrieval of things you already know, which is its own skill.

Pair it with spacing

Retrieval says how to review: by producing, not recognising. Spacing says when: later than you want to. Together they form the core loop: test yourself on fading material, fail a little, recover, repeat at a longer interval. The details are in the spaced repetition article; the combination is worth more than any specific resource you will ever buy.
