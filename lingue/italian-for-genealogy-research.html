Italian for Genealogy: how to read birth, marriage and death records

You do not need to speak Italian to read an Italian birth record. You need about forty words, and to know that one of them changes the meaning of the whole document.

The forty words that do the work

Civil records are formulaic. The same sentences repeat for a century, which is excellent news: learn the formula once and you can read every record in the register.

l’atto di nascitabirth record
l’atto di matrimoniomarriage record
l’atto di mortedeath record
lo stato civilethe civil registry
il comunethe municipality — the unit that holds the records
la provinciathe province
l’ufficiale dello stato civilethe registrar
la levatrice / l’ostetricathe midwife, who often reports the birth
il testimone / i testimonithe witness / witnesses
il contadinofarmer — by far the commonest occupation you will meet
la casalingahousewife
il possidentelandowner
domiciliato inresiding in
nato / nata inborn in
coniugato / coniugata conmarried to
vedovo / vedova diwidower / widow of
gli allegatithe attached documents, often the richest part
l’anno millenovecento…the year nineteen… — dates are written out in full

The one word that changes everything

In front of a father’s name you will find either di or fu.

  • di Giuseppe — son or daughter of Giuseppe, who was alive when the record was made.
  • fu Giuseppe — son or daughter of the late Giuseppe.

That is two letters, and it can tell you that a man died between one child’s birth and the next. Miss it and you will spend a month looking for a death record in the wrong decade.

Numbers and dates

Ages and dates are written as words, not digits: di anni trentadue, il giorno quindici del mese di marzo. Learning the numbers to one hundred is the single highest-return hour you can spend — see Italian numbers 1 to 100.

Where the records are

Italian civil registration begins in 1866 for most of the country, and earlier in the South, where Napoleonic administration introduced it around 1809. Before that you are in parish registers, in Latin — a different problem.

Many registers have been digitised by the State Archives and published on the national Portale Antenati. Records that are not online are held by the comune, and writing to a comune in Italian gets an answer far more often than writing in English.
