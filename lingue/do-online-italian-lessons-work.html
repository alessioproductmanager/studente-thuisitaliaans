Do online Italian lessons really work?

Short answer: for most adults, yes, at least as well as in-person lessons, on two conditions. Here is the honest version, from someone who has taught both since 2019.

Where the doubt comes from

The scepticism is reasonable. Apps promised fluency in three weeks and delivered streaks. Group video courses during the lockdowns left many people with Zoom fatigue and a half-finished workbook. So when someone asks me whether online lessons “really” work, they are usually asking: is this another screen thing that goes nowhere?

It helps to separate three things that all get called “online lessons”: self-study apps, recorded video courses, and live lessons with a teacher who sees you, hears you and corrects you in real time. They are not the same product. This article is about the third one, because that is the one I teach.

What the research actually says

I wrote my second master's thesis on the online language class, at Università Giustino Fortunato, and the starting point was Krashen's affective filter: anxiety and embarrassment block language learning before any grammatical difficulty does. That finding matters here, because a one-to-one video lesson lowers the filter for many adults. You are in your own room. No classmates are listening. A mistake stays between two people and gets fixed in ten seconds.

The second pillar is interaction: language grows in conversation where meaning gets negotiated, where you ask, misunderstand, repair and try again. A video call carries that kind of conversation perfectly well. What no study says is that a screen teaches by itself. The variable that predicts progress is not the room, physical or virtual. It is what happens in the hour: how much you speak, how you are corrected, and whether the lesson connects to the one before it.

What a good online lesson looks like

Concretely, a good online lesson is a conversation with tools around it. We talk in Italian as much as your level allows. The text or exercise is on a shared screen, so we are literally looking at the same thing. The chat window becomes an instant glossary: every new word I type stays there, and you keep the whole list after the lesson, spelled correctly, in the order it came up. Corrections go in the chat too, while you keep speaking, so the flow is not interrupted every time an article is wrong.

Homework comes out of what actually happened in the lesson, not from a generic page of a workbook. If you struggled with the passato prossimo in minute twenty, that is what lands in your inbox afterwards.

Where online wins

  • No commute. An hour of lesson costs an hour, not two and a half. That difference decides whether a busy adult is still learning in March.
  • Rhythm is easier to keep. Weekly regularity predicts progress better than talent does, and it is much easier to protect a slot that does not involve traffic.
  • Everything leaves a written trace. The chat, the shared documents, the corrections: after six months you have your own personal archive.
  • Time zones stop being a problem. I teach from the Netherlands, and my students connect from wherever they are. The lesson moves with you when you travel.
  • You choose the teacher, not the postcode. If nobody in your city teaches Italian well, that no longer matters.

Where in-person still wins

Honesty is part of the answer. Online is a poor fit for young children, who need movement and physical play to stay in a language. It is frustrating if your connection is unstable, because a lesson that freezes twice per sentence trains patience, not Italian. And a classroom course has a social side, the coffee and the classmates, that a video call does not replace. If that social side is your main reason for learning, a local group course is a fine choice, and I say so in trial lessons when it is true.

The two conditions

So: online Italian lessons work on two conditions.

First, the lesson must be live and interactive. You should be speaking for a large part of the hour. If you spend the lesson listening to explanations you could have watched on YouTube, the format is wasted.

Second, the days between lessons must not be empty. One hour a week is less than one percent of your week. The students who progress fastest touch Italian for fifteen or twenty minutes on the other days: a chapter of a graded reader, a round of free exercises, a stage of an app that follows a real CEFR path. That is exactly what the lesson hour cannot give you, and the lesson gives what no app can: someone who hears your Italian and adjusts to it.

Common questions

How many online lessons per week do I need?

For most adults, one is enough, on the condition that the days in between are not empty. Fifteen to twenty minutes of daily contact with Italian beats a second weekly lesson, and costs nothing.

What do I need technically?

A laptop or tablet, a stable connection and a quiet corner. We use Zoom or Google Meet; I send the link, you click it. If you can manage a video call with family, you can manage a lesson.

I am a complete beginner. Isn't online too hard for a first lesson?

No. The first lesson is spent listening to you and finding your starting point, not testing you. Beginners are the students for whom the low-anxiety side of online works best.
