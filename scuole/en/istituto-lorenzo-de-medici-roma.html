What is worth knowing before you book

These are the same questions for every school, and no website ever explains them in full. Here are the answers once, so that when you write to the school you only ask what is genuinely missing.

The price is never just the course

On top of the weekly rate there is almost always a one-off enrolment fee, typically around €50–90 in Italy. Then these may be charged separately: books and materials, accommodation, the accommodation-finding service, cultural activities and the exam fee. Always ask for the total, not the weekly price.

Twenty hours are not twenty hours

Almost every school counts in 'lesson hours' of 45 or 50 minutes, not 60. A 20-hour week with 45-minute lessons is 15 real hours; with 50-minute lessons it is 16 hours 40. The actual lesson length is listed under 'The course' on this page: use it to compare two schools at the same price.

How long it takes to move up a level

The most common yardstick is roughly 200 guided lesson hours to move from one Common European Framework level to the next. At an intensive 20 hours a week that is about ten weeks per level, less if you also study outside class. One or two weeks will not change your level: they unlock your spoken Italian, which is already a lot.

Which certificate to choose

The four recognised by the Italian state are CILS (University for Foreigners of Siena), CELI (University for Foreigners of Perugia), PLIDA (Società Dante Alighieri) and CERT.IT (Roma Tre University). They are worth the same: what differs is the exam format and the session dates. Italian citizenship requires level B1; enrolling at an Italian university usually requires B2. AIL is a private certificate, widespread but not on the state list.

Visa, permit and paperwork

With an EU passport you need nothing but your ID card. From outside the EU, many nationalities may stay up to 90 days in any 180 in the Schengen area without a visa; beyond 90 days you need a study visa, and to get it the school must send you an enrolment letter with the course already paid. Within eight days of arrival you apply for the residence permit. Start at least two months ahead.

Accommodation: the three options

Host family: the most useful for the language, often with breakfast or half board, and it makes you speak Italian every evening. Shared flat with other students: more freedom, less language practice if your flatmates speak English. Studio or residence: convenient and pricier. Always ask the distance from the school, what is included, whether there is a deposit, and which day to which day the week runs.

Five questions to ask before you pay

How many people are actually in class in high season, not the stated maximum. How long a lesson lasts in minutes. What the total is including enrolment, materials and accommodation. What happens if few people sign up at your level: is the class merged, reduced to fewer one-to-one hours, or cancelled. What the cancellation terms are and whether the deposit is refundable.

Studying in Roma: what nobody tells you

Where it is worth staying

Almost all the schools are inside or just outside the Aurelian Walls. San Lorenzo and Pigneto are the student districts, cheap and lively at night; Trastevere and Monti cost more but are walkable; Prati is quiet and well connected. Avoid taking a room outside the ring road: you save little and lose an hour a day.

Getting around

Three metro lines (A, B, C) plus trams and buses, all on the same time-based integrated ticket. The metro covers less than you would expect from a capital: many trips are made by bus or on foot. Weekly and monthly passes exist: if you stay longer than two weeks, ask the school which one is worth it, as they change often.

The Italian you will hear

Romanesco is not a separate dialect but a strong accent with its own vocabulary: 'ammazza', 'daje', 'sto a fa'' instead of 'sto facendo'. You will hear it constantly in bars and on buses, and at first it is confusing. It is excellent training for real listening, a long way from textbook Italian.

When to book

Rome is busy all year, but the critical weeks are around Easter, the 25 April long weekend and the months from June to September. In those periods courses and accommodation sell out one or two months ahead. November, January and February cost less, classes are small and the city is yours.

What not to miss in Roma

  1. Colosseo e Fori ImperialiThe centre of the ancient world, walkable in half a day. Its official name is the Flavian Amphitheatre: 'Colosseum' comes from the colossal statue of Nero that once stood beside it.
  2. Musei Vaticani e San PietroThe Sistine Chapel and the dome: always book ahead. The Swiss Guard has served here without interruption since 1506, and is still made up solely of Swiss citizens.
  3. Pantheon e TrastevereThe Rome you live in the evening, between squares and trattorias. The Pantheon's dome, in Roman concrete, is still after two thousand years the largest in the world without steel reinforcement.
Best airport: Roma Fiumicino (FCO)

Events this year

The city's big festivals, food fairs and cultural seasons through the year. They're not just entertainment: they're the best training ground for your Italian. At a local festival you end up ordering, asking directions, chatting with locals — the real language, the kind books don't teach. If you can, line up a week of your course with one of these.

Natale di Roma
On 21 April Rome celebrates its founding (753 BC) with costumed parades, historical re-enactments at the Forums and fireworks at the Circus Maximus. A living immersion in Roman history: excellent for historical vocabulary and for seeing how much Romans love their city.
21 April
Estate Romana
From June to September Rome fills with open-air cinema, concerts along the Tiber and neighbourhood festivals. The long evenings are the ideal moment to practise Italian outside the classroom, ordering an ice cream or chatting at a free concert.
June–September
Festa del Cinema di Roma
In October the Auditorium hosts premieres, directors and actors from all over the world. Even without a ticket the city breathes cinema: perfect listening practice with the Italian of red carpets and interviews.
October
Internazionali di tennis
In May the Foro Italico hosts one of the most important tournaments in the world. Sports vocabulary, numbers and live crowd support turn these days into a laboratory of colloquial Italian.
May
Sei Nazioni di rugby
Between February and March Rome hosts Italy's home matches. Pubs and the stadium fill with fans: an informal setting, perfect for picking up exclamations, chants and everyday Italian.
February–March

Frequently asked questions

When do Italian courses at Istituto Lorenzo de' Medici Roma start?
Courses start every Monday. Complete beginners: fixed start dates, usually every 2 weeks. Always confirm the next available date using the form on this page.
How many lesson hours per week are there?
The intensive course runs about 20 hours per week; the lighter option about 10 hours, ideal if you want time to enjoy the city.
How much does an Italian course in Roma cost?
As a rule of thumb, an intensive group course in Italy costs €200–300 per week. For the exact price ask the school directly with the form on this page — they reply to your email.
Does the school help with accommodation?
Yes: Istituto Lorenzo de' Medici Roma offers a housing placement service (free). Ask about availability and prices together with course dates.
Can I prepare for or take an official exam (CILS, CELI, PLIDA)?
For CILS, CELI or PLIDA certificates, ask the school: many prepare students for the exam and point you to the nearest exam centre.
How do I get to Roma?
The recommended airport is Roma Fiumicino (FCO); from there take a train or bus to Roma. See the city guide above for the key landmarks.
Good to know before you enrol
Lessons last 45 minutes and classes have up to 12 students, so you speak a lot from day one. On your first day you take a placement test to join the right level. Bring an ID and, if you have one, a previous level certificate. Book a few weeks ahead in summer, when classes fill up.
