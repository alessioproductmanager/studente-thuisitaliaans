Regional variation

by Alessio · teacher of Italian as a foreign language, ITALS master's

Standard Italian lives in books; in the streets lives the Italian of the regions: anguria in Milan, cocomero in Rome, melone d'acqua in Sicily. C2 does not judge: it recognises where a sentence speaks from, and knows that at the table geography is heard more than anywhere else.

Regional does not mean wrong: it means situated. In a job interview the standard passato prossimo is safer; at a Sicilian dinner, the host's remoto is local music. C2 means knowing where you are.

The most common mistakes

  • il cocomero è il nome sbagliato dell'anguria → they are regional names for the same thing: neither is wrong
  • dire "la Giulia" è un errore di articolo → it is a Northern regional trait, informal but alive
  • a Palermo parlano male perché usano il passato remoto → in Palermo the remoto in speech is the local norm

Frequently asked questions

Are Italian dialects corrupted Italian?

No: they are Latin's other children: Sicilian, Venetian, Neapolitan and the rest evolved directly from spoken Latin in parallel with Florentine, which became the standard for literary prestige, not linguistic superiority. Many have their own literature: sisters of Italian, not its mistakes.

Why do some Italians say la Giulia, with the article?

Northern regional habit: the article before female first names (la Giulia, la Franca) is normal from Lombardy to Tuscany, in some areas for male names too (il Marco). The standard omits it: in formal writing, plain Giulia: at a Milanese dinner table, la Giulia is simply how she is called.

Is scendi il cane wrong?

It is Southern regional Italian: transitive scendere and salire (scendi il cane, sali la spesa) are widespread in the South, listed in dictionaries as regional, and famously discussed by the Crusca. In local informal speech they work: in exams, formal writing and the North, keep portare giù / portare su.

Did you know?

At unification in 1861, by Tullio De Mauro's famous estimate, only about 2.5% of the population could speak Italian: other scholars raise it to roughly ten: everyone else lived in dialect. The national language spread through schools, conscription, internal migration and, decisively, television: Italian became the mother tongue of the majority only within living memory.
