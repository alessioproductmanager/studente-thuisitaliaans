Subject pronouns

by Alessio · teacher of Italian as a foreign language, ITALS master's

In Italian the verb already tells you who is speaking: parlo can only be io. That is why subject pronouns are often dropped, and using them constantly sounds odd: you add them for clarity or emphasis.

The table

PersonPronounExample
1a sing.ioio sono di Roma
2a sing.tutu sei Sofia Loren?
3a sing.lui / leilui è Marcello Mastroianni, lei è Anna Magnani
1a plur.noinoi siamo attori
2a plur.voivoi siete registi
3a plur.loroloro sono famosi

When to use them

CaseExample
Contrastoio prendo il caffè, lui il tè
Enfasil'ha detto lei, non io
Dopo anche / neancheanche noi amiamo Fellini
Da soli, senza verboChi è stato? Io.
The polite Lei: formal address uses the third person, even when speaking to a man: Lei è molto gentile, dottore.

The most common mistakes

  • io parlo, io mangio, io vado ogni frase → in Italian the pronoun is dropped: parlo, mangio, vado
  • Lui è venuto? (parlando col dottore) → Lei è venuto?, formal address uses the third person

Try it now

Is the pronoun needed? Choose:

  1. amo i film di Sophia Loren.
  2. Chi ha scelto il ristorante? , come sempre.
  3. prendo la pizza, la pasta: gusti diversi.
  4. Scusi, è il professor Rossi?

Frequently asked questions

When do I use tu and when Lei?

Tu with family, friends, children, and generally among younger people; Lei with strangers, in shops and offices, and with anyone older or in a professional role. When in doubt, start with Lei: the other person will offer the switch with diamoci del tu.

My textbook has egli and ella: do people say that?

No: egli, ella, esso, essa belong to literary and bureaucratic writing. Spoken and normal written Italian uses lui, lei, loro as subjects: lui arriva domani. You should recognise egli in old books, not produce it.

Is voi ever used as a polite form?

As a plural, voi is simply “you all”, always correct. As a polite singular it survives in parts of the South and in old-fashioned commercial letters, but the modern standard for formal address is Lei.

Did you know?

The polite Lei was born in the Renaissance from expressions like Vostra Signoria (“your lordship”): a feminine noun, which is why the pronoun is grammatically feminine and third person even when you address a man. You are not calling the doctor “she”: you are addressing his invisible lordship.
