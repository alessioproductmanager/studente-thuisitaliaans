Waar gaat Il Milione over?

In 1271 vertrekt de jonge Marco Polo uit Venetië met zijn vader en oom, beiden kooplieden, richting het Oosten. Na jaren reizen over de Zijderoute bereikt hij het hof van Kublai Khan, de Grote Khan van de Mongolen, die hem bijna twintig jaar in dienst neemt.

Woestijnen, sprookjesachtige steden, papiergeld, stenen die branden: Marco dicteert zijn herinneringen aan zijn medegevangene Rustichello da Pisa, in een cel in Genua. Zo ontstond het beroemdste reisboek van de middeleeuwen, deels kroniek, deels wonder.

Waarom Il Milione lezen om Italiaans te leren

De korte hoofdstukken werken als reiskaarten: elk een stad, een volk, een wonder — perfect voor gerichte leessessies. De woordenschat van aardrijkskunde en handel is nuttig en concreet, en de passato remoto wordt telkens in heldere context geïntroduceerd.

In de woordenlijst

Woordenlijst

De kernwoorden van de roman, op alfabet. Eerst de Italiaanse definitie, dan de Engelse, dan wat het woord in dit verhaal betekent.

Cambaluc · Cambaluc, Khanbaliq, Beijing (sin. Khanbaliq, Pechino)
Nome medievale di Khanbaliq — «la città del Kane» —, la capitale imperiale che sorgeva dove oggi è Pechino.
Medieval name of Khanbaliq, the imperial capital that stood where Beijing is today.
In the novel: Kublai had it built in stone as a sign of his power. Marco describes it square, with twelve gates, huge markets, a palace at the centre. It is not a steppe city: it is a sedentary capital that a nomad wanted, to leave a trace. Kublai’s paradox in a single name.
Catai · Cathay, northern China (sin. Cina del nord, Catay)
Nome medievale della Cina del nord, sotto il dominio mongolo. Deriva dai Khitani, popolo di steppa che governò la regione prima dei mongoli.
Medieval name of northern China under Mongol rule; became for Europe a synonym for China.
In the novel: Cathay for Marco is the country of Kublai, his seat of power. For Europe it becomes synonymous with China. For centuries cartographers will draw «Cathay» to the north and «Mangi» — southern China — as two different countries. They were one and the same: only, Marco had known them separated.
Chandu · Xanadu, Shangdu (sin. Xanadu, Shangdu)
Xanadu, la residenza estiva di Coblai. Un palazzo di canne dorate, smontabile, dentro un parco murato di quindici miglia.
Kublai’s summer residence, a palace of gilded canes within a walled park of fifteen miles.
In the novel: In the West the name has become legend: Coleridge in 1797, in a dream, turned it into poetry. Marco had seen it for real — white horses, hunting with the leopard, the ceremony of milk poured to the wind for the spirits. The first Xanadu in European history is his.
Cinghis Kane · Genghis Khan (sin. Gengis Khan, condottiero)
Il primo grande Kane dei mongoli, fondatore dell’impero (1162 ca.–1227). Nel testo italiano medievale il nome ha molte grafie: Cinghis, Cinghiscan, Chinghis.
The first great Khan of the Mongols, founder of the empire (c. 1162–1227).
In the novel: Marco cites Genghis as the ancestor of Kublai: from him comes the empire the book describes. By the thirteenth century his figure was already legend, and Marco receives him as a told story — he never saw him. The many spellings in the medieval text (Cinghis, Cinghiscan, Chinghis) show a name travelling by ear, from mouth to mouth, before it ever reached paper.
Cipangu · Cipangu, Japan (sin. Giappone, Zipangu)
Nome con cui Marco chiama il Giappone, terra insulare a oriente della Cina che lui non visita ma di cui gli parlano.
The name Marco gives to Japan, an eastern island of which he heard but never visited.
In the novel: Marco describes it at second hand: he says the palace roofs are covered in gold, the inhabitants eat pearls and use red and white. It is the chapter that Christopher Columbus will read two centuries later, and believing he can reach it across the Atlantic, will find America.
Cocacin · Kokachin, the Mongol princess (sin. principessa, sposa)
Principessa mongola, ultima protagonista del viaggio.
A Mongol princess, the last protagonist of the journey: Marco escorts her by sea from Kublai to the lord of Persia.
In the novel: She is the reason for the journey home: Marco, his two uncles and seven hundred men escort her by sea from Kublai to the lord of Persia. The book closes on her delivery. History confirms her: Persian sources record the arrival of the princess with the Polos — one of the few points where the Milione can be checked, and it holds.
