An honest comparison

App, language school, marketplace or private lessons?

Four ways to learn Italian, with their real strengths and weaknesses — including the cases where you don't need me at all.

Take the first step →︎

This is the page where I explain why you might work with me. I've tried to be honest about it, because the alternative — claiming to be best at everything — convinces nobody anyway.

An app like Duolingo

Apps are better than their reputation suggests. They keep you going, they're free or cheap, and for building basic vocabulary they're excellent. Anyone who claims you learn nothing from them has never used one seriously.

Where it goes wrong is production. An app checks whether you tap the right answer. It doesn't hear you swallow the double consonant, it doesn't notice that you avoid the congiuntivo by rebuilding your sentences, and it doesn't correct the mistake you've been repeating for six months without knowing.

Take an app if you are just starting out, only want to build vocabulary, or want to practise between two lessons.
Don't take an app if you have been at it for months and notice that speaking isn't keeping up.

A group course at a language school

Good language schools do good work, and a group has things a private lesson doesn't: fellow students, a fixed structure, a lower price per hour, and the social pressure that gets you out of the house on a dark Tuesday evening in November.

The two limitations are structural. The first is speaking time: in a group of twelve you get a few minutes per lesson. The second is the pace: it's the average pace of the group. On top of that, courses start on fixed dates and the offer often stops around B2.

Take a group if you are a beginner, want to keep it sociable, care about budget, and September suits you anyway.
Don't take a group if you have a deadline, want to get past B2, or work irregular hours.

A marketplace like italki or Preply

Plenty of choice, a low barrier to entry, and you really do find good teachers there — I know, because I work on those platforms myself. If you want pure conversation hours at a sharp price, this is a fine choice.

The weak point is continuity. You book single hours, often with changing teachers, usually without an ongoing programme. One of my students put it like this:

“Unlike many italki teachers, whose lessons often lack structure, Alessio is very organised.”

What I do differently

There are two things you don't get from the other three. Neither of them is “personal attention” — everybody says that.

1. I'm trained in teaching Italian, not just in Italian

I'm a native speaker, but that's not the point: there are plenty of us. I hold two master's degrees specifically in the teaching of Italian to speakers of other languages — Università Ca' Foscari Venezia (ITALS) and Università Giustino Fortunato. That's a profession, and it's a different profession from speaking the language.

2. I write my own material

Almost every language school and every platform works from a commercial coursebook, written for an average student who doesn't exist. I don't use a coursebook.

  • Over 700 interactive exercises — free, no account.
  • My own iOS app with a full CEFR path from A1 to C2.
  • Italian classics, rewritten by level — with notes and a glossary.

When you don't need me

If you just want a few words for your holiday: get an app. If what you mainly want is good company and a low price per hour: take a group course. If you want pure speaking hours with no programme: a marketplace is cheaper than I am. And if you'd rather carry on by yourself, the exercises and the app are there whether or not you ever take a lesson with me.

When I am the right choice

  • You're stuck at B1 or B2: you understand a lot, but speaking isn't keeping up.
  • You want to get past B2, and what's on offer in your area stops there.
  • You have a concrete deadline: a move, a certificate, a client in Milan.
  • You work irregular hours and don't fit a fixed timetable.
  • You want one teacher who remembers your path, instead of separate hours.

Who I am

I'm Alessio, Italian, and I live in Ede. I've been teaching Italian for more than six years and have worked with over thirty students.

I hold two master's degrees specifically in teaching Italian to speakers of other languages: one at Università Ca' Foscari in Venice (ITALS), one at Università Giustino Fortunato. That's a different thing from speaking Italian as a native. It means I know why Dutch speakers trip over the congiuntivo, why the double consonant is so hard for a Dutch ear, and in what order to introduce things so that they stick.
