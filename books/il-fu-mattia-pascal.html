CAPITOLO 1

Io mi chiamo Mattia Pascal

My Name Is Mattia Pascal

Una delle poche cose che sapevo di me era questa: che mi chiamavo Mattia Pascal.

E mi bastava.

Quando un amico veniva da me a chiedere un consiglio, io alzavo le mani e rispondevo:

«Io mi chiamo Mattia Pascal.»

«Grazie. Questo lo so.»

«E ti sembra poco?»

Non sembrava molto, a dire il vero. Neanche a me.

Ma allora non sapevo ancora che cosa vuol dire non poter più rispondere così.1

1 Il libro comincia con un uomo che dice: «l'unica cosa che so di me è il mio nome». E finirà con un uomo che va al cimitero a leggere quel nome sulla propria lapide. Fra queste due frasi c'è tutto Pirandello — e lui te lo dice alla prima riga, senza nascondere niente.

The plot of Il fu Mattia Pascal

Crushed by an unhappy marriage and an unbearable mother-in-law, Mattia Pascal runs away from home. In Monte Carlo he wins a fortune at roulette and, on the train home, reads in the paper that he is «dead»: a body has been identified as his.

Free of everything, Mattia becomes Adriano Meis and starts over in Rome. But a man without a past can have neither love nor rights: absolute freedom turns out to be a prison. The novel that made Pirandello famous throughout the world.

Why read Il fu Mattia Pascal to learn Italian

The puzzle plot — a man believed dead who invents a new life — is a magnet: you want to know how it ends, and meanwhile you read. Mattia's short reflections introduce the present subjunctive in crystal-clear contexts.

Inside the glossary

Glossary

adulterio · adultery ((sin. infedeltà, tradimento coniugale))
Il tradimento fra marito e moglie.
Betrayal between husband and wife.
When Mattia comes back, Romilda has another husband. If he is alive, she is an adulteress; if he is dead, she is respectable. The law has no box to tick for a man who is both at once.
biblioteca · library ((sin. libreria, raccolta di libri))
Il luogo pieno di libri dove si va a leggere.
The place full of books, where one goes to read.
The Boccamazza library is an empty church with two thousand books and no readers. Mattia spends his days watching the rats eat them, and does nothing to stop it: a portrait of a wasted life.
cadavere · corpse ((sin. corpo, salma))
Il corpo di una persona morta.
The body of a dead person.
The body in the mill-race is a stranger, and it becomes Mattia Pascal because a woman points at it. This is where the novel states its thesis outright: you are whoever other people say you are.
caso · chance ((sin. sorte, combinazione))
Quello che succede senza che nessuno l'abbia deciso.
What happens without anyone having decided it.
Pirandello doesn't believe in destiny; he believes in chance, which is far worse. Destiny has a design — chance has none. This entire novel starts because a man gets off a train because he is hungry.
colpa · guilt ((sin. responsabilità, peccato))
La responsabilità di aver fatto qualcosa di grave.
The weight of having done something seriously wrong.
Mattia's guilt isn't that he ran away: it is that he never did anything. «And I did nothing» repeats through the book like a sentence handed down — and the man handing it down is always himself.
debito · debt ((sin. obbligo, passivo))
Il denaro che si deve a qualcuno.
Money that is owed to someone.
Debt is the chain of Mattia's first life. When he learns he is dead, his very first thought is that the debts now belong to somebody else — to a man who does not exist.
denaro · money ((sin. soldi, moneta))
I soldi.
Money.
For Mattia money is never wealth: it is distance. He wins eighty-two thousand lire, and his first thought is not «I'm rich» but «now I can buy my way out».
denuncia · report to the police ((sin. accusa, querela))
L'atto di dire alla polizia che qualcuno ha commesso un reato.
Telling the police that someone has committed a crime.
The report Adriano cannot make is the exact centre of the novel. The thief is standing in front of him, he knows his name, he knows what was taken — and he cannot open his mouth. That is what total freedom costs.

About the author: Luigi Pirandello

Luigi Pirandello (1867–1936), winner of the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1934, revolutionised the novel and the theatre with his themes of identity, masks and madness. Il fu Mattia Pascal is his best-loved novel.
