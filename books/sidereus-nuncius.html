CAPITOLO 1

L’annuncio

The Announcement

Mi chiamo Galileo Galilei e insegno matematica all'università di Padova. Nel marzo del 1610 ho pubblicato a Venezia un piccolo libro in latino, scritto in poche settimane.1 L'ho chiamato Sidereus Nuncius: l'annuncio che viene dalle stelle. Non è un libro di teorie. È il racconto di ciò che ho visto con i miei occhi, notte dopo notte, attraverso uno strumento nuovo: il cannocchiale. E ciò che ho visto, lo dico subito, è più grande di qualunque teoria. Prometto al lettore cose che nessun essere umano ha mai osservato prima: le annuncio qui, e poi le racconto una per una, perché ogni scoperta merita la sua storia.

stella = star · cannocchiale = telescope, spyglass · scoperta = discovery

1 Il titolo latino è ambiguo, e forse di proposito: «nuncius» significa sia «messaggero» sia «messaggio». Galileo intendeva «l'annuncio delle stelle», ma mezza Europa lesse «il messaggero delle stelle» — e il soprannome, più bello del titolo, gli rimase addosso per sempre.

The plot of Il Sidereus Nuncius

Padua, summer 1609. A professor of mathematics hears of a Dutch tube with two lenses that brings distant things closer. He has never seen one: he rebuilds it by reasoning, improves it to thirty magnifications — and then does what no one had seriously done before: he points it at the sky.

The Moon, which every book called smooth and perfect, fills with mountains and valleys. And on the night of 7 January 1610, three small stars appear in a line beside Jupiter… and the next night they are no longer where they should be. The book that founded modern astronomy, told in Galileo's own voice, night after night, down to the race to print it before anyone else.

Why read Il Sidereus Nuncius to learn Italian

This is the Italian of science told in the first person: observing, doubting, deducing, proving — the thinking verbs a B2 learner needs to build arguments. And the story, night after night, has the suspense of a mystery: you know he will discover Jupiter's moons, but not how.

Inside the glossary

Glossary

cannocchiale · telescope, spyglass ((sin. telescopio, occhiale))
Strumento ottico a tubo, con due o più lenti, che ingrandisce gli oggetti lontani.
An optical tube with lenses that makes distant objects appear closer.
In the novel: Galileo did not invent it — a Dutch spectacle-maker did — but he was the first to turn it into a scientific instrument: he worked out the optics, pushed it from three magnifications to thirty, and pointed it upward. The book insists on this order of events: the tool was common merchandise; the idea of aiming it at the sky was the discovery before the discoveries.
eclissi · eclipse ((sin. oscuramento, occultamento))
L'oscuramento temporaneo di un corpo celeste, coperto da un altro o dalla sua ombra.
The darkening of a celestial body when another body or its shadow hides it.
In the novel: The four satellites disappear on schedule: behind Jupiter, into its glare, or into its shadow — small eclipses, cousins of our lunar ones. Their punctuality is the point: what vanishes and returns on a timetable is not an illusion but a mechanism, and mechanisms can be understood.
lente · lens ((sin. vetro, cristallo))
Disco di vetro curvo che devia i raggi di luce e forma immagini ingrandite o rimpicciolite.
A curved piece of glass that bends light rays passing through it.
In the novel: Two curved pieces of glass, one convex and one concave, in a lead tube: the whole revolution rests on them. The book is honest about their limits — poor glass, narrow field, trembling images — and about the craft: Galileo ground and polished lenses himself until his instruments were the best in Europe.
luce · light ((sin. chiarore, splendore))
La radiazione che rende visibili le cose; il chiarore che viene dal Sole o dagli astri.
The radiation that makes things visible to the eye.
In the novel: Light is the book's raw material, and Galileo treats it like a physicist: he traces where it comes from, where it bounces. His most beautiful piece of reasoning explains the ashen glow of the dark Moon as earthshine — our planet lighting its companion — making the Earth a luminous body among the others.
luna · moon ((sin. satellite, astro))
Il corpo celeste che gira attorno alla Terra e ne illumina le notti con luce riflessa.
The celestial body that orbits the Earth and shines at night with reflected light.
In the novel: The Moon is the book's first battlefield. Ancient philosophy required it to be a perfect, polished sphere; the telescope shows mountains, valleys and plains, «just like the face of the Earth». If the closest heavenly body is made of the same rough stuff as our world, the wall between Earth and sky has already cracked.

About the author: Galileo Galilei

Galileo Galilei (1564–1642), from Pisa, father of modern science, was the first to point a telescope at the sky and publish what he saw. The Sidereus Nuncius of 1610 suddenly made the universe much bigger.
