Era primavera, e a Parigi c'era una grande festa.

Il re Carlo Magno aveva invitato tutti i cavalieri del mondo.

Erano arrivati cristiani e saraceni, amici e nemici. Per la festa, la guerra si fermava.

The plot of L'Orlando innamorato

Paris. King Charlemagne has invited every knight in the world — friends and enemies — to a great tournament. Then a girl nobody has ever seen walks into the hall: her name is Angelica, she comes from the East, and she is the most beautiful woman in the world. Within one evening, every knight is in love. Even Orlando, the strongest warrior alive, who has never loved anyone.

What follows is a chase across the world: enchanted forests, magic fountains that swap love and hate, a golden lance that never loses, giants, wizards and dragons — while armies larger than any city come from the sea. The poem that taught Italy how to tell adventures, openly continued by the Orlando furioso.

Why read L'Orlando innamorato to learn Italian

Fantasy adventure before fantasy existed: duels, wizards and dragons keep the pace racing, and at A2 pace is everything. The narrative structures repeat (challenge →︎ duel →︎ spell) and become natural scaffolding for comprehension.

Inside the glossary

Glossary

amore · love ((sin. passione, affetto))
Il sentimento fortissimo che lega una persona a un'altra.
The very strong feeling that binds one person to another.
The force that moves the whole book — Boiardo put it in the title. But here love is never chosen: it arrives like a spell, in a single moment, and turns the strongest warrior in the world into a man who cannot sleep. Nobody is safe from it — not kings, not sages.
anello · ring ((sin. cerchietto, vera))
Piccolo cerchio di metallo che si porta al dito.
A small metal circle worn on the finger.
Angelica's magic ring: worn, it breaks every enchantment; held in the mouth, it makes her invisible. It is the single most powerful object in the poem — and it belongs to the person everyone is chasing. Ariosto inherited it, ring and all, for the Furioso.
armatura · armour ((sin. corazza, difesa))
Il vestito di ferro che protegge tutto il corpo del guerriero.
The iron clothing that protects the warrior's whole body.
The iron suit that makes a knight a fortress — and, once, a monster: Rodamonte scales the walls of Paris in armour made of dragon scales, drinking arrows like rain. Armour in this poem tells you who a man is before he says a word.
assedio · siege ((sin. accerchiamento, blocco))
L'accerchiamento di una città per costringerla ad arrendersi.
Surrounding a city to force it to surrender.
The siege is the poem's favourite shape: tents covering the earth to the horizon, a fortress breathing inside. Albracca first, then Paris — same image, sides reversed. Whoever besieges will be besieged: Boiardo builds his symmetries like an architect.
battaglia · battle ((sin. combattimento, scontro))
Scontro armato tra due eserciti.
An armed clash between two armies.
The great battle under the walls of Paris gathers every living character onto a single plain — and never ends. Every sword stops in mid-air, forever, because history interrupted the poet. No battle in literature has a stranger fate: it is still being fought.
battesimo · baptism ((sin. sacramento, rito))
Il rito con l'acqua che fa entrare una persona nella fede cristiana.
The rite with water that brings a person into the Christian faith.
Agricane, dying, asks his enemy for baptism with the water of the fountain beside them. It is the most solemn page of the poem: two enemies, one dawn, one gesture that turns a duel into something older and larger. Boiardo, who jokes about everything, does not joke here.

Language focus: what you practise

In this level-A2 edition you mainly practise imperfetto versus passato prossimo, the simple future, direct and indirect pronouns. The vocabulary revolves around knights, magic and adventure: the key words, with translations, are in the glossary below and recur throughout the text, so you learn them in context.

About the author: Matteo Maria Boiardo

Matteo Maria Boiardo (1441–1494), count of Scandiano and poet at the court of Ferrara, invented the great Italian chivalric romance with L'Orlando innamorato. His poem breaks off in mid-verse, as «Italy goes up in flames» in the French invasion.
