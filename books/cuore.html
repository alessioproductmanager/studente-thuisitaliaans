CAPITOLO 1

Il primo giorno di scuola

The First Day of School

Oggi è il primo giorno di scuola.

Le vacanze sono finite. Io non sono contento.

Mia madre mi porta a scuola. Io penso alla campagna. Io penso agli alberi e ai monti.

Nella strada ci sono molti bambini. Ci sono molte madri e molti padri.

Davanti alla porta c'è tanta gente. Noi entriamo con fatica.

Io ho dieci anni. Adesso vado in terza, in una classe nuova.1

◆

Vicino alla porta un uomo mi tocca la spalla.

È il mio maestro dell'anno scorso. Ha i capelli rosso.

Il maestro dice: «Enrico, adesso siamo separati.»

scuola = school · fatica = hard work, toil · classe = class, classroom · maestro = teacher

1 Enrico ha dieci anni e scrive un diario. Tutto il libro è scritto da lui: quello che leggi è quello che vede un bambino. De Amicis finge di essere un ragazzino di terza — ed è per questo che le frasi sono corte.

The plot of Cuore

Turin, late nineteenth century. Enrico Bottini, a third-grade pupil, fills his diary with a whole school year: his classmates, his teacher Perboni, the small conflicts and great friendships that grow between the desks. Around him is newly unified Italy, with its social differences and its ideals.

Woven into the diary are the famous monthly tales, such as the little Florentine scribe or Marco's journey from the Apennines to the Andes: episodes of courage and generosity that have moved generations of Italian readers.

Why read Cuore to learn Italian

The diary format breaks the novel into short episodes: five-minute readings, perfect for daily practice at A1. The vocabulary of school, family and emotions is exactly what you use in your first conversations, and the monthly tales give you complete little stories within the story.

Inside the glossary

Glossary

addio · farewell ((sin. saluto, commiato))
La parola che si dice quando si va via per sempre.
The word you say when you leave for ever.
In the novel: Cuore ends with a goodbye, not a celebration. School closes, the boys scatter, the master is left alone. De Amicis refuses to hand you a happy ending: he says that good things end, and that it hurts. Nobody had ever dared say that to children before.
aiutare · to help ((sin. soccorrere, dare una mano))
Fare qualcosa per una persona che ha bisogno.
To do something for a person who needs it.
In the novel: This is the verb of the book. Not «love», not «understand»: HELP. Nobody in Cuore makes speeches about feelings — they hand over their bread, lend their notebook, drag a child out from under a cart. Goodness here is something you do with your hands.
bugiardo · liar ((sin. falso, menzognero))
Chi dice le bugie.
Someone who tells lies.
In the novel: The children in this book lie often — and almost always to protect somebody. Giulio lies to his father for a whole year, and does it out of love. De Amicis doesn't condemn that lie: he tells it, and makes us weep.
castigo · punishment ((sin. punizione, pena))
La punizione che si dà a chi ha fatto una cosa sbagliata.
What is given to someone who has done wrong.
In the novel: Punishment almost never arrives. The schoolmaster could punish and doesn't; the injured old man could have Garoffi arrested and forgives him instead. Chapter after chapter, De Amicis says the same thing: trust achieves more than punishment ever will.
cattivo · bad, wicked ((sin. malvagio, crudele))
Chi fa del male agli altri, e lo fa apposta.
Someone who hurts others, and does it on purpose.
In the novel: There is exactly one villain in this book: Franti. And De Amicis does something almost nobody does — he doesn't forgive him, doesn't explain him, doesn't redeem him at the end. Franti is bad on the last page too, and his story ends badly.
classe · class, classroom ((sin. aula, scolaresca))
La stanza dove i bambini studiano, e i bambini che stanno dentro.
The room where children study, and the children in it.
In the novel: In this book the class isn't a room, it's a small city. There are rich boys and poor boys, strong and weak, the good ones and Franti. The whole of Italy fits inside four walls — and De Amicis did that on purpose.
compagno · classmate, companion ((sin. amico, collega))
Il bambino che sta in classe con te.
The child who is in your class.
In the novel: The most important word in the book. In Italian it means two things at once: the person beside you, and the person on your side. Enrico's classmates aren't friends — they simply landed in the same room. The novel is about how you become friends anyway.

In this level-A1 edition you mainly practise the present tense, the passato prossimo, basic articles and prepositions, short coordinated sentences. The vocabulary revolves around school, family and emotions: the key words, with translations, are in the glossary below and recur throughout the text, so you learn them in context.

About the author: Edmondo De Amicis

Edmondo De Amicis (1846–1908) was a journalist, travel writer and one of the best-loved voices of post-unification Italy. With Cuore he created the most widely read children's book in Italian literature, translated into dozens of languages.
