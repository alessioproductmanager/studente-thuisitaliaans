Italian Grammar in Context: The Noticing Hypothesis

12 June 2026

A grammar rule you meet on a chart is a fact. The same rule noticed inside a story you were following is an experience. They do not get stored the same way, and the difference explains one of the oldest frustrations in language learning: knowing the rule perfectly and never using it right.

The theoretical backbone here is Richard Schmidt's noticing hypothesis (1990): input becomes intake, raw material your acquisition can actually use, only when you consciously notice the form in it. Not analyse, not parse: notice. You are reading a Deledda story, you have seen era and andò living side by side for three chapters, and one day the difference between the background tense and the event tense simply surfaces. That moment of noticing, Schmidt argued from his own diary studies of learning Portuguese, is where grammar acquisition actually begins.

Texts first, rules second

The practical consequence inverts the textbook: encounter grammar in meaningful texts before studying it in rules. This is not a fringe idea; it is the working method of the ITALS school of Italian teaching at Ca' Foscari in Venice, where I did my training: units organised around meaning (semantic fields, real texts), with grammar drawn out of the texts rather than parachuted in ahead of them. The sequence matters because the rule then arrives as an explanation of something you have already met and half-noticed, and explanations of familiar experiences stick. The rule met first, in a vacuum, has nothing to attach to.

For self-study: when a structure starts catching your eye in your reading, that is the day to open the grammar chapter about it. Curiosity-ordered grammar study beats chapter-ordered grammar study, because the noticing has already happened.

The spiral: same grammar, new contexts, never new grammar

The second half of the method is what happens after the first meeting, because one meeting settles nothing. A spiral syllabus revisits the same grammar repeatedly, at intervals, in fresh contexts, which quietly combines spacing and interleaving at the scale of the whole course. The discipline is in what the revisits do not contain: a proper review station adds no new grammar at all. Old structures, new situations. Review that smuggles in new material is not review; it is a new lesson wearing a review's badge, and it robs the old grammar of the consolidation it came for.

How this is built into ti

The course structure of ti is this article in software. Units are organised by semantic field in the ITALS manner, with grammar met inside texts and dialogues before the per-level grammar book explains it. The "Facciamo il punto" stages, thirty of them across the A1–C2 path, are pure spiral: the same grammar in new contexts, with a hard rule that they never introduce anything new. And when an exercise corrects you, the explanation stays attached to the sentence through every later review, so the noticing moment travels with the error, the mechanism described in learning from your mistakes.

None of this makes grammar study unnecessary; it makes it land. Meet it in the wild, then read the field guide, then meet it again somewhere new.
