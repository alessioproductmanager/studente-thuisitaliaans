Italian B1 Writing: What Examiners Expect

12 July 2026

Examiners do not expect literature. They expect a letter that does its job: the right register, the required points covered, readable Italian of the length requested. I have seen candidates with lovely Italian lose points for ignoring the task, and candidates with modest Italian pass comfortably because they answered exactly what was asked. Writing is the most coachable section of the B1 exam, and this is the coaching.

What the task really asks

B1 writing tasks are practical: a message to a friend, a short letter to an office, a description of an experience, typically with a required length and a list of points to include. The grading rewards four things, in roughly this order: task completion, so every requested point appears; appropriateness, so a formal letter sounds formal and a message to a friend does not; organisation, so the text has a beginning, a middle and an end with connectors doing the joining; and accuracy, where errors are tolerated at B1 as long as they do not block understanding. Notice what is missing from that list: brilliance. The exam wants function, and function can be trained.

How to train writing alone

Two short pieces a week beat one long Sunday essay, for the same spacing reasons that govern everything else in learning; see the spacing article if you want the science. Train with constraints that copy the exam: a topic, required elements, a word range, a time limit. Build a personal bank of connectors and formulas, because half of a B1 letter is reusable machinery: openings, closings, transitions, polite requests. Learn these as whole chunks, the way I describe in learning Italian in chunks, and the exam letter starts assembling itself. Then reread your own texts two days later and correct what you now see; your own recurring errors are the syllabus, as always.

Where guided writing fits

The ti app trains this loop deliberately. The guided writing exercise gives you a prompt, required words and a minimum length, shows a model answer only after you have written, and saves your texts into a portfolio, so by exam week you own a folder of your real production, which is also precisely the evidence habit I recommend in the self assessment article. The 35 B1 mock tests include writing tasks in exam format, so the constraint training above happens inside real simulations. One honest boundary, as ever: the app checks the requirements of your text, not its grammar, by design, because a wrong automatic verdict is worse than none. For grammatical correction of free writing, a human still wins; bring two of your texts to a teacher and you will fix more in an hour than in a month alone. The full plan that this section slots into is in the preparation guide.

Note and attributions: task types and grading criteria are set by the certifying bodies and vary between CILS, CELI, PLIDA and Cert.it; the description above is a teacher’s generalisation, so always study the official sample papers of your chosen exam.
