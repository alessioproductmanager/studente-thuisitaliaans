Phones in the Italian Classroom: A GDPR-Proof Language Lab

21 July 2026

Every teacher knows the fight against phones in class. Fifteen students, fifteen screens, one losing battle for attention. The alternative to fighting the phones is giving them a job, and a language classroom is one of the few places where the device in every pocket genuinely can become lab equipment, if two conditions hold: the activities are real teaching, not screen-time with a syllabus sticker; and the app clears the privacy bar that a classroom of minors legally and morally demands. This article takes both seriously.

Three phone activities that earn their minutes

The two-minute warm-up. Start the lesson with a timed blitz on already known material, the fluency practice principle. In ti this is the "Ripasso lampo": two minutes, only previously met items, phones down when the timer ends. It settles the room, activates last week's Italian, and its strict no-new-material rule means nobody is lost in minute one.

The class game. A timed competition on level material works with groups in a way it rarely does one-to-one, and the leaderboard question is real: some classes thrive on it, some students suffer under it. It should be the teacher's switch, per class, not the app's default. In ti it is exactly that: disattivabile.

Asynchronous homework for online groups. For evening courses and online classes, assignments by tappa between sessions, with completion and real error patterns visible to the teacher, turn the week between lessons from a void into the spaced practice the research orders. The mechanics are the class-code system described in the private-teacher article; with fifteen students instead of one, the error overview stops being convenient and becomes the only way to know where the group actually stands.

The GDPR questions to ask any classroom app

Schools in the EU are data controllers for their students, and an app in the classroom is a processing decision. Before any tool touches your class, ti included, the questions to ask its maker, in writing:

  • What data leaves the device? The best answer is: as little as possible. ti runs its exercises, speech recognition and review system on-device by design; there is no advertising, no data sale, and the student speaks to their phone, not to a server, which I consider a didactic argument, not just a legal one.
  • Is there a real student/teacher separation? Linking a student to a teacher should require explicit action on both sides; in ti, consent gates stand between a class code existing and a teacher seeing anything.
  • What about minors? Age-appropriate design is not optional. ti ships parental controls with a PIN gate and age bands, so schools and parents can restrict social features (high-fives, leaderboards) for younger students.
  • Can data be deleted on request? The GDPR answer must be yes, simply, without archaeology.

Any vendor who answers these with marketing instead of specifics has answered them.

The honest close

Phones will not teach your class Italian; you will. What they can do (warm-up, game, spaced homework, an error map of the whole group) is give you back preparation time and information you did not have. Fifteen phones, one job each. If you want the quick-start version for a first class, the setup in the teacher article takes an evening, and teacher mode is free.
