Reading I Promessi Sposi in Italian: a realistic plan

26 July 2026

Ask an Italian which novel defines their literature and you will get one answer. Ask them whether they enjoyed reading it at sixteen and you will get a different one. Both facts matter if you are planning to read it.

I promessi sposi is the book Italians quote without noticing. Don Abbondio's cowardice, the Innominato's conversion, Perpetua, the plague in Milan, questo matrimonio non s'ha da fare. If you have ever felt that a conversation in Italian was referring to something you could not place, there is a fair chance it was this.

Why the original defeats most learners

Manzoni wrote it three times. The 1840 version, the one everyone reads, was deliberately written in a Florentine Italian he had gone to Tuscany to acquire, because he was trying to invent a national literary language for a country that did not yet exist.

That is a magnificent thing to know and a difficult thing to read. The sentences are long and hypotactic, the vocabulary is nineteenth century, and the famous opening description of Lake Como goes on for pages before anything happens.

At B1 or B2 in the original you will spend forty minutes on two pages and put it down. That is not a failure of will. It is a mismatch of level.

Three ways in, ranked

An adapted edition at your level. The plot, the characters and the structure survive; the syntax is brought down to where you are. You read the whole book instead of the first chapter. This is what I would do, and it is why I made a B2 version.

A dual-language edition. Original on one page, English on the other. Excellent for savouring the prose, poor for building fluency: your eye slides right and you stop working. Useful later, as a second pass.

Read it in translation first, then in Italian. Unfashionable advice that works. Knowing the plot frees all your attention for the language. Nobody is grading you on the order.

A plan that finishes

Thirty eight chapters in the original, twenty in the adapted version. At two chapters a week you finish in ten weeks.

Read each chapter twice: once for the story, without stopping, then once for the language. The second pass takes half the time. Do not look up every unknown word; look up the ones that come back three times.

And keep a note of the sentences you like. Manzoni's irony is the reason Italians still quote him, and it survives adaptation better than his descriptions do.

What you get that a course cannot give you

Two things. The first is the references: after this book, a whole layer of Italian conversation opens up.

The second is subtler. Manzoni is writing about power, and specifically about ordinary people with no protection against it. Don Rodrigo is not a monster, he is a local man with connections. That is a very Italian book, and reading it changes how you hear a lot of other things.

If you finish it

Go to Pirandello. Il fu Mattia Pascal is shorter, funnier and psychologically modern, and after Manzoni it will feel easy. Both are in the graded series, and the whole reading order by level is in this article.
