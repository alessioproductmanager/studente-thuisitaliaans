Italian CEFR Levels Explained: What A1 to C2 Really Mean

8 January 2026

B1 is not a grade, a percentage, or 500 hours logged in an app. It is a list of things you can do in Italian: hold your own in conversation on familiar topics, handle most situations travelling, describe experiences and give brief reasons for your opinions. The CEFR (Common European Framework of Reference, QCER in Italian) is the map behind those letters everyone throws around, and once you read it as can-do statements instead of jargon, it becomes genuinely useful for planning your learning.

The six levels in real-life terms

A1: you survive politely. Introduce yourself, order at the bar, ask where the station is, understand slow careful speech aimed at you. "Un caffè e un cornetto, per favore" territory, and already worth having.

A2: you handle the routine. Shopping, directions, simple sentences about your family, your job, your weekend. The past tense arrives and suddenly you can tell what happened.

B1: you are independent. The threshold level: conversations on familiar ground, travel without rehearsing sentences in advance, a simple connected story about your life. This is also the level Italian citizenship applications require (the full picture is in the B1 citizenship exam guide), which makes it the most searched letter of the six.

B2: you argue. "Ordino al bar" was A1; "difendo la mia opinione" is B2. You follow native speed, discuss abstract topics, notice your own mistakes, read ordinary novels without a graded edition. Most "fluent" claims by honest people are B2.

C1: the language stops being the obstacle. Long implicit texts, flexible professional and social use, humour, register. Hard, unhurried work gets here; ads do not.

C2: near-native precision. Rarer than the certificates on LinkedIn suggest, and not required for any normal life goal.

Finding your level without flattering yourself

Two honest instruments exist. The first is self-assessment against the official can-do descriptors, skill by skill (listening, reading, spoken interaction, spoken production, writing), because levels are jagged: a B1 reader with A2 speaking is normal, and the profile tells you what to train. That method gets its own article: can-do self-assessment.

The second is a placement test built from real level material. In ti the entrance placement is adaptive A1→C2: five questions per level drawn from the actual tappe of the course, climbing until your errors accumulate, and stopping there: a free level test that takes minutes and lands you where the material genuinely fits. Mid-level checkpoints and a final test with an 80% threshold then verify the level as you climb, rather than assuming it.

A note on honest certification

One thing I insist on saying plainly, because the app-certificate economy does not: an in-app certification, including ti's, is an internal attestation, not an official CILS or CELI certificate. For citizenship or university you need the official exams from the certifying institutions. What a serious app can honestly give you is the preparation, the level verification along the way, and a portfolio of your real written work as evidence of where you stand. Anyone promising more than that on a phone screen is selling paper.
